Polynomial interpolation

Definition 4.1 Polynomial interpolation

Let FF be a field and D≥0D\ge 0 an integer. Given D+1D+1 pairwise distinct points a0,…,aD∈Fa_0,\ldots,a_D\in F and values b0,…,bD∈Fb_0,\ldots,b_D\in F, their interpolating polynomial is the unique P∈F[t]P\in F[t] of degree at most DD such that P(ai)=biP(a_i)=b_i for every 0≤i≤D0\le i\le D. Lagrange’s formula gives

P(t)=∑i=0Dbi∏0≤j≤Dj≠it−ajai−aj.P(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{D} b_i \prod_{\substack{0\le j\le D\\j\ne i}}\frac{t-a_j}{a_i-a_j}.

To check Lagrange’s formula, we evaluate it at a sample point aka_k. In the term indexed by kk, we get 11 from every fraction, since the numerator and denominator are both ak−aja_k-a_j. In each term indexed by i≠ki\ne k, we get zero from the factor t−akt-a_k. Hence at aka_k we recover exactly bkb_k.

Vandermonde interpolation algorithm

To compute the coefficients of PP, we write P(t)=c0+c1t+⋯+cDtDP(t)=c_0+c_1t+\cdots+c_Dt^D. The input consists of two vectors aa and bb, where aia_i is the ii-th point and bib_i is the value prescribed there. We seek the coefficient vector cc.

a=(a0a1⋮aD),a=\begin{pmatrix}a_0\\a_1\\\vdots\\a_D\end{pmatrix},
b=(b0b1⋮bD),b=\begin{pmatrix}b_0\\b_1\\\vdots\\b_D\end{pmatrix},
c=(c0c1⋮cD).c=\begin{pmatrix}c_0\\c_1\\\vdots\\c_D\end{pmatrix}.

When we evaluate PP at aia_i, we obtain a linear equation in its coefficients. For instance, at a0a_0 we have c0+a0c1+⋯+a0DcD=b0c_0+a_0c_1+\cdots+a_0^Dc_D=b_0. If we put these equations in order, row ii contains the powers of aia_i from zero through DD. Hence we obtain the system Vc=bVc=b with Vandermonde matrix

V=(1a0a02⋯a0D1a1a12⋯a1D⋮⋮⋮⋱⋮1aDaD2⋯aDD).V=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & a_0 & a_0^2 & \cdots & a_0^D\\ 1 & a_1 & a_1^2 & \cdots & a_1^D\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 1 & a_D & a_D^2 & \cdots & a_D^D \end{pmatrix}.

To prove uniqueness, we take zz in the kernel of VV and form Q(t)=∑j=0DzjtjQ(t)=\sum_{j=0}^D z_jt^j. In row ii of Vz=0Vz=0 we have Q(ai)=0Q(a_i)=0. Thus we have found D+1D+1 distinct roots of a polynomial of degree at most DD. Over a field this forces Q=0Q=0, so every coefficient zjz_j is zero. Hence VV has full column rank. Since VV is square, we conclude that it is invertible.

  1. In row ii of VV we start with 11 and multiply by aia_i for each successive entry.

  2. Next we solve Vc=bVc=b by Gaussian elimination over FF.

  3. From cc we recover P(t)=∑j=0DcjtjP(t)=\sum_{j=0}^D c_jt^j. If we only need P(0)P(0), we return the first coefficient c0c_0.

We can also read the condition on the sample points from the determinant. In det⁡V=∏0≤i<j≤D(aj−ai)\det V=\prod_{0\le i<j\le D}(a_j-a_i), a factor vanishes precisely when two points coincide. To fill in VV we use O((D+1)2)O((D+1)^2) field operations, and to solve the system we use O((D+1)3)O((D+1)^3).

As an example, suppose P∈ℚ[t]P\in\mathbb Q[t] has degree at most two and we know its values 6,11,186,11,18 at 1,2,31,2,3, respectively. The three equations for its coefficients are

(111124139)(c0c1c2)=(61118).\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&4\\1&3&9\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}c_0\\c_1\\c_2\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}6\\11\\18\end{pmatrix}.

We eliminate c0c_0 by subtracting the first equation from the second and the second from the third. This leaves us with c1+3c2=5c_1+3c_2=5 and c1+5c2=7c_1+5c_2=7. Subtracting once more, we get 2c2=22c_2=2, so c2=1c_2=1. We then substitute back to find c1=2c_1=2 and c0=3c_0=3. Hence P(t)=3+2t+t2P(t)=3+2t+t^2. In particular, we have recovered P(0)=3P(0)=3 without sampling at zero.

Suppose now that we also know P′(1)P'(1). From the value at 11 we obtain c0+c1+c2=P(1)c_0+c_1+c_2=P(1), and from the derivative we obtain c1+2c2=P′(1)c_1+2c_2=P'(1). Thus we get two different equations from the same point. In the next lemma we extend this observation to several points and higher derivatives.

Lemma 4.2 Hermite interpolation

Let FF be a field of characteristic zero, let a1,…,aℓ∈Fa_1,\ldots,a_\ell\in F be distinct, and let h≥1h\ge 1 be an integer. If P∈F[t]P\in F[t] has degree at most D<ℓhD<\ell h, then the values P(k)(ai)P^{(k)}(a_i) for 1≤i≤ℓ1\le i\le\ell and 0≤k<h0\le k<h uniquely determine PP. Here P(0)=PP^{(0)}=P. Two polynomials with the same data differ by a multiple of ∏i=1ℓ(t−ai)h\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(t-a_i)^h. This product has degree ℓh\ell h, so two such polynomials of degree at most DD must coincide.

In the main proof, F=ℚF=\mathbb Q, ai=ia_i=i, and D=ndD=nd. Solving the resulting linear system for the coefficients recovers P(0)P(0) in time polynomial in the bit length of the interpolation data.

At each point aia_i, for i=1,…,ℓi=1,\ldots,\ell, we have hh values, one for each derivative order 0,…,h−10,\ldots,h-1. We collect them in the column vector bib_i.

a=(a1a2⋮aℓ),a=\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\\\vdots\\a_\ell\end{pmatrix},
bi=(P(ai)P′(ai)⋮P(h−1)(ai)).b_i=\begin{pmatrix} P(a_i)\\P'(a_i)\\\vdots\\P^{(h-1)}(a_i) \end{pmatrix}.

With v(t)=(1,t,…,tD)v(t)=(1,t,\ldots,t^D), we have P(t)=v(t)cP(t)=v(t)c. Since the entries of cc are constants, differentiating gives us P(k)(t)=v(k)(t)cP^{(k)}(t)=v^{(k)}(t)c. For a fixed ii we evaluate at aia_i for each order 0,…,h−10,\ldots,h-1, obtaining the equations Hic=biH_i c=b_i. Stacking one block per point, we obtain the system Hc=bHc=b.

Hi=(v(ai)v′(ai)⋮v(h−1)(ai)),H_i=\begin{pmatrix} v(a_i)\\v'(a_i)\\\vdots\\v^{(h-1)}(a_i) \end{pmatrix},
(H1H2⋮Hℓ)(c0c1⋮cD)=(b1b2⋮bℓ).\begin{pmatrix}H_1\\H_2\\\vdots\\H_\ell\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}c_0\\c_1\\\vdots\\c_D\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\\\vdots\\b_\ell\end{pmatrix}.

If we number the rows and columns of HiH_i from zero, we can find entry (k,j)(k,j) by differentiating tjt^j exactly kk times and evaluating at aia_i. We obtain j!aij−k/(j−k)!j!\,a_i^{j-k}/(j-k)! when k≤jk\le j, and zero when k>jk>j.

As in the proof for VV, we take a vector zz in the kernel and form Q(t)=∑j=0DzjtjQ(t)=\sum_{j=0}^D z_jt^j. From Hiz=0H_i z=0 we know that Q(ai),Q′(ai),…,Q(h−1)(ai)Q(a_i),Q'(a_i),\ldots,Q^{(h-1)}(a_i) all vanish. Expanding QQ in powers of t−ait-a_i makes use of all hh equalities. The coefficient of (t−ai)k(t-a_i)^k is Q(k)(ai)/k!Q^{(k)}(a_i)/k!. Here we use the assumption that the field has characteristic zero, so we can divide by k!k!. The first hh coefficients vanish, and hence (t−ai)h(t-a_i)^h divides QQ.

At every aia_i we obtain one such factor. Since the points are distinct, the factors (t−ai)h(t-a_i)^h are pairwise coprime. Therefore we have a divisor of QQ of degree ℓh>D\ell h>D, namely the product of all these factors. Since deg⁡Q≤D\deg Q\le D whenever QQ is nonzero, we must have Q=0Q=0 and hence z=0z=0. This gives us rank⁡H=D+1\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}H=D+1. Here HH may have more rows than columns. Full column rank tells us there is at most one solution, and we know there is one because the given data came from PP.

For example, suppose we know the value and first derivative of a polynomial of degree at most three at two distinct points a1,a2a_1,a_2. From these data we obtain four equations

(1a1a12a13012a13a121a2a22a23012a23a22)(c0c1c2c3)=(P(a1)P′(a1)P(a2)P′(a2)).\begin{pmatrix} 1 & a_1 & a_1^2 & a_1^3\\ 0 & 1 & 2a_1 & 3a_1^2\\ 1 & a_2 & a_2^2 & a_2^3\\ 0 & 1 & 2a_2 & 3a_2^2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}c_0\\c_1\\c_2\\c_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}P(a_1)\\P'(a_1)\\P(a_2)\\P'(a_2)\end{pmatrix}.

For the first two rows we evaluate PP and P′P' at a1a_1. The second row starts with zero because c0c_0 drops out when we differentiate. We repeat the calculation at a2a_2 for the last two rows. In the main proof we use the derivative data from Lemma 3.3 in the same way. Once we solve the system, we can read P(0)=per⁡(A)P(0)=\mathop{\mathrm{per}}(A) from its first coefficient.
